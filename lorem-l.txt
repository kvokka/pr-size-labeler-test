The wind never really stops in the Westfjords.
It just changes its mind about how hard it wants to push.
Einar noticed it most in late March when the light returned
but still carried the memory of winter like a bruise.
He was seventy-one now, or maybe seventy-two—he had stopped counting.
The calendar on the wall was three years behind anyway.
This morning the sea was the color of wet slate.
Gulls screamed overhead like they were personally offended by the dawn.
He pulled the wool cap lower and stepped onto the path.
The black stone was still there, half-buried in new moss.
He never touched it anymore.
He only nodded once, the way you nod to an old neighbor.
Today the path felt longer than usual.
Or maybe his knees were simply older.
Halfway to the boat he heard it again—the low hum.
Not loud.
Just present.
Like someone breathing beside him.
He kept walking.
The boat waited, tarpaulin stiff with salt and frost.
He folded it back slowly, joints complaining.
Inside lay the same net, the same knife, the same thermos.
Routine is a kind of religion when you live alone.
He pushed off.
The water was mirror-flat for once.
Reflections of cliffs doubled the world.
Somewhere under the surface the cod were moving in slow silver rivers.
He dropped the line.
Waited.
The hum followed him even here.
Faint through the hull.
He poured coffee from the thermos.
It was already lukewarm.
A puffin landed on the gunwale, ridiculous orange beak glowing.
Einar looked at it.
The bird looked back, unafraid.
“You’re late,” he muttered.
The puffin tilted its head, then flew off.
Nothing bit for almost an hour.
He didn’t mind.
Waiting was most of fishing anyway.
Around noon the first cod came up.
Fat, surprised, flapping once before going still.
He thanked it quietly.
Old habit from his father.
Another followed.
Then another.
By three o’clock the hold was respectable.
Enough for the week, maybe more.
The sky had gone the color of old pewter.
Snow coming, probably.
He turned the boat toward shore.
The engine coughed once, then settled.
Halfway back the hum grew louder.
Not angry.
Just… insistent.
Einar cut the motor.
The boat drifted.
Silence pressed in from every direction.
Then a voice—soft, neither man nor woman.
“You kept the promise.”
He looked around.
Nothing but water and rock.
“I said I would,” he answered.
A ripple moved across the surface.
Not wind.
Something underneath.
Large.
Slow.
Then it was gone.
He started the engine again.
The hum faded back to its usual place.
When he reached the beach the snow had begun.
Big soft flakes that melted on his sleeves.
He tied the boat.
Carried the cod up in two trips.
Inside the house he lit the stove.
The room smelled of woodsmoke and damp wool.
He cleaned the fish at the sink.
Blood swirled pink down the drain.
Later he sat with coffee and the radio.
Static mostly.
A weather report in Icelandic he half understood.
Storm coming tomorrow.
He nodded to himself.
The stone on the windowsill caught the last gray light.
It looked almost warm.
He didn’t speak to it tonight.
Some nights he did.
Some nights it answered.
Tonight was quiet.
Good quiet.
The kind that lets you hear your own heartbeat.
He finished the coffee.
Put the cup in the sink.
Outside the snow was thickening.
Covering the path.
Covering the stone.
Covering everything.
Einar went to bed early, listening to the wind remember its old songs.
